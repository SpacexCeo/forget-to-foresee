# Game-jam and indie-festival judging, onboarding and the first ten minutes

Read on 2026-11-08 (UTC). Juror notes feeding `docs/jury/rubric.md`.

## Summary for the rubric
- Jurors give a game minutes, not hours. An IGF judge aimed for 20 to 30 minutes per game but says peers want to be hooked "within the first 2 minutes"; an LD post-mortem says most raters spend about 15 seconds before deciding. Score the opening as the whole game, because for most judges it is.
- Festival criteria converge on the same four words: innovation, quality, impressiveness, enjoyability (IGF Grand Prize). Nuovo and IndieCade add "substantive risks" and innovation; A MAZE adds "forward thinking, genre pushing". Fun is never optional; novelty is the tiebreaker.
- Ludum Dare rates 1 to 5 stars in Overall, Fun, Innovation, Theme, Graphics, Audio, Humor, Mood; Overall is a separate vote, not an average. Entries with fewer than 20 ratings are not ranked, so discoverability and a browser build are part of the score.
- Jam reviewers prefer "a game with simpler mechanics that is well-polished and has good instructions" over an ambitious idea the rater cannot parse in a few minutes. Fun beats scope.
- Onboarding orthodoxy (Fan, Valve, Portal, GMTK): teach by doing, one concept at a time, at most about eight words on screen, hints only for players who are stuck, and make the world answer every player action.
- Playtesting rule everyone repeats: watch silently, give no hints. Confusion observed in a test is a design bug. Valve shipped when "playtesting was no longer painful to watch".
- Demos: developers design 30-minute demos but the measured median is 14 minutes; a median under 10 minutes points at the opening, not the length. Zukowski: a demo is "a playable trailer", not the first 30 minutes.
- Strategy-game critics score decision density, transparent information, outcomes that visibly follow from choices, no busywork, and "easy to learn, hard to master" elegance. Koster: fun is pattern learning; a game that is too chaotic or too quickly mastered bores.
- For a ten-minute session the checklist below asks: goal in 30 seconds, first real decision by 2 minutes, visible cause and effect, signature mechanic reached, readable text, graceful failure, no dead time.

## Findings

### Ludum Dare rules (2017 archive) and community practice
- Source: https://ludumdare.com/resources/archive/rules-2017/ (read 2026-11-08). The live `/rules/` page returned 404 on this date, so the archived rules are the official text available.
- Eight rating categories: Innovation ("the unexpected, or something unique or notably different"), Fun ("how much you enjoyed playing"), Theme, Graphics, Audio, Humor, Mood ("storytelling, emotion, and vibe"), Overall ("your overall opinion, across every aspect important to you").
- Scale is 1 to 5 stars per category, or N/A. "All participants that submit a game are allowed to judge." Compo games are "typically reviewed harsher than Jam games."
- Theme is a single category, not a gate: entries are not disqualified for ignoring it, but the community prefers games that use it.
- Coolness: raters who rate more games get their own game shown to more raters. Visibility is earned by playing others' entries.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludum_Dare (read 2026-11-08). After the deadline participants "are given three weeks to play and rate other submitted games to determine the winners." Compo is 48 hours solo with all assets made during the event; Jam is 72 hours, teams allowed.
- Source: https://itch.io/jam/cgj/topic/511972/minimum-number-of-ratings (read 2026-11-08). Community statement of the threshold: "Ludum dare has a minimum of 20 votes to qualify"; other jams copy it because otherwise "you see a lot of games in top spots with <5 ratings."
- Source: https://itch.io/post/897247 (read 2026-11-08). LD lets voters give an Overall rating separate from category scores; a plain mean of categories understates how much a voter liked a game.
- Source: https://csanyk.com/2012/09/5-stars-of-ambiguity (read 2026-11-08). A long-time LD rater on how stars are read: ratings are "an imperfect tool", the "aggregated consensus of our individually limited opinions"; "Asteroids has 5-star graphics, too" (cohesive style beats fidelity); games are rated "as games", not as 48-hour projects; silence can be a deliberate audio choice.

### Ludum Dare post-mortems: what scores
- Source: http://makenai.net/Ludum-Dare-38-Postmortem/ (read 2026-11-08; the page returned 503 twice, points below are from the search-engine snippet of it). Advice: optimize for fun in the first 15 seconds "since that's how much most people spend"; funny or joke games tend to do well.
- Source: https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/playing-and-rating-game-jam-games/ (read 2026-11-08). A rater's view after an itch jam: "It's essential that you are able to understand how to play the game quickly"; "a game with simpler mechanics that is well-polished and has good instructions" usually beats a more ambitious one; complicated mechanics that cannot be grasped in a few minutes hurt; slow pacing is called out by name.
- Source: https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2262922-ludum-dare-38-post-mortem (read 2026-11-08). An in-game tutorial "gave a player perfect idea of how to progress through game"; no feedback that "players had no idea what to do". Simpler concept than the previous entry. WebGL build ran badly, which cost ratings.
- Source: https://gamedeveloper.com/design/ludum-dare-entry-fun-facts-and-advices (read 2026-11-08). "I'm a coder, not an artist": pick a style you can make look intentional; "try to hide your weakness by making the strongest elements of the game on the front"; teach an upgrade by flying into a zone so players "see and feel directly what the upgrade does".

### Independent Games Festival: categories and process
- Source: https://igf.com/about-igf/ (read 2026-11-08). Seumas McNally Grand Prize ($10,000) is "overall innovation, quality, impressiveness, and enjoyability"; Excellence in Design covers mechanic design, level design and difficulty balance; Visual Art is "quality and impressiveness of each Entered Game's appearance and visual effects"; Audio, Narrative, Character are $2,000 each; Nuovo ($2,000) is for games that take "substantive risks" or an unusual approach to game making; Best Student Game is "overall quality, impressiveness, and enjoyability". Judges are "a diverse, industry-leading group of industry professionals".
- Source: https://igf.com/submit-your-game (read 2026-11-08). Judge pool of "250-300 participants", each game to a random set of judges matched to platforms they own, "roughly 3-5 weeks" to play and recommend. Each category jury has "7-10 members"; approval voting picks finalists, a second round picks the winner. Only the build "first uploaded by the deadline" is guaranteed to be seen. Complicated games may attach a public or private tutorial video. Written judge feedback has been removed.
- Source: https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2014/02/igf-game-submission-thoughts-and-advice.html (read 2026-11-08). A first-year judge: at least 25 games per judge, "easily more than 10 hours". Heading: "Be interesting within the first 15 minutes"; "some judges would argue, 'be interesting within the first 2 minutes'"; he "tried really hard to give each game at least 20-30 minutes". Description: "a short sentence or two"; anything you want noticed, "just put it in your game". Broken builds: "after some point, we just give up". Keyboard and mouse support so a second judge can try. Goal: "help judges play your game so we can talk about it!"
- Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/igf-some-tips-if-you-want-to-submit-a-game (read 2026-11-08). "we have hundreds of games to consider in only 3 weeks"; one judge tested a game for only a few minutes; "Offer builds with cheat codes" because judges "won't have time to reach the most interesting parts of your game"; tell judges how long the game takes; the readme must state "the game's current state" and controls; slow downloads and internet-only web builds make judges skip; "Take 10 minutes to compare your game to last year's finalists."

### IndieCade
- Source: https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/how-the-indiecade-jurying-process-works (read 2026-11-08; Kris Ligman, 2013, two-time juror). About 800 submissions; each entry to at least two jurors; jurors play "ideally to completion, or at least long enough to get a good feel for the mechanics"; scoring on "world design, innovation and even cultural significance" plus a written review. Advice: avoid clones, keep instructions short ("if you can't explain your game simply" it fails with attendees), keep the narrative layer thin, "don't submit a broken build".
- Source: https://www.indiecade.com/submissions-help-section/jurying-process/ (read 2026-11-08; page body did not render, points from search snippet). Round one reviews the documentation package; jurors play in later rounds; assignment by juror qualifications and hardware; a Jury Committee (chairs, Festival Director, Festival Chair) picks finalists. Director quote: "We look for innovation first and foremost, and there are other judging criteria".

### A MAZE., Day of the Devs, The Game Awards
- Source: https://2025.amaze-berlin.de/award-winners/ (read 2026-11-08). 364 works from 72 countries, 30 nominated by a selection committee, an independent jury honoured "the most forward thinking, genre pushing works" with five awards (Most Amazing, Digital Moment, Human Human Machine, Long Feature, Explorer) plus WINGS and an Audience Award. No numeric rubric is published.
- Source: http://www.dayofthedevs.org/submit (read 2026-11-08). Curators use a "mysterious algorithm" whose listed factors are: what is new and exciting about the game, studio background or unique perspective, completeness and playability, a build playable in full or as a demo, a release date, prior showcases, asset deadlines. Required assets include a 2 to 3 minute intro video, gameplay footage, fact sheet, screenshots.
- Source: https://thegameawards.com/faq (read 2026-11-08). Winners are a blended vote, jury 90% and fan vote 10%; the jury is more than 100 media and influencer outlets, each submitting an unranked top-five ballot per category; the five games on the most ballots are nominees. No written criteria for Best Independent Game are published.

### George Fan, "How I Got My Mom to Play Through Plants vs. Zombies" (GDC 2012)
- Source: https://gdcvault.com/play/1015541/contactUs (read 2026-11-08). Session listing: how he got his mom "to play through a full-blown strategy game while not alienating hardcore gamers."
- Source: https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-10-tutorial-tips-from-i-plants-vs-zombies-i-creator-george-fan (read 2026-11-08). The ten tips: blend the tutorial into the game ("We strive to make it not feel like a tutorial at all"); have players do, not read ("The best way for a player to learn is to actually perform actions in the game"); spread out mechanics (money mattered only at level 10); let players perform an action once ("Once they see the results of their action, that's often all it takes"); fewer words ("There should be a maximum of eight words on the screen at any given moment"); unobtrusive messaging that does not pause play; adaptive messaging for struggling players only ("give players the chance to feel smart if they're already doing the right thing"); don't create noise ("like being the little boy who cried wolf"); use visuals to teach (Peashooter's mouth); leverage what people already know (coins mean value).
- Source: https://uxdesign.cc/lessons-in-onboarding-from-plants-vs-zombies-d936d58c10c (read 2026-11-08, from search). Fan's claim that without the tutorial "the other elements of the game wouldn't have mattered" for a casual player.

### Valve: Half-Life Cabal, Half-Life 2 design process, Portal
- Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-cabal-valve-s-design-process-for-creating-i-half-life-i- (read 2026-11-08; Ken Birdwell). Valve "decided to start over and rework every stage of the game"; "experiential density" is events per unit of time and map area, triggered by distance so the player never waits long; playtest observers "were not allowed to give any hints or suggestions"; about 100 action items per session, 200+ sessions; instrumented logs found stretches "too long without any encounters"; "if the world ignores the player, the player won't care about the world"; failure should feel like the player's fault, with danger hinted and a way out offered.
- Source: https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2006/GDC2006_HL2DesignProcess.pdf (read 2026-11-08; text extracted locally). "Playtests are your experiments"; simulate the player "in their living room": "Don't give them hints", "Don't answer any questions"; "Often you learn more from what playtesters don't experience"; loop: "Create 15 minutes of gameplay in rough form", playtest, "Use playtest to prioritize work for next week"; "We felt done as soon as playtesting was no longer painful to watch"; "Don't worry about how it looks" because art is less risky than gameplay.
- Source: https://gmtk.substack.com/p/valves-secret-weapon (read 2026-11-08; Mark Brown, 2023). Valve "typically tests every single week"; Portal tested one week into development with a half-finished room; rule "Shut up and watch"; "you'll often learn more by watching players than by talking to them"; lessons "players don't learn when stressed" and "players don't look up"; a tester spent half an hour pushing a shelf and ignored the box, which produced Portal's clean white look; the gravity gun was moved earlier because players loved it; testers' suggested fixes are data, not instructions.
- Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success (read 2026-11-08). Kim Swift: playtesting was "probably the most important thing we did on Portal"; the first room's force field was changed to glass because players did not understand it; the world was made "cleaner and simpler to help teach the players what they need to know"; after each test players retold the story, and failure to do so flagged inattention; a complex boss "would only slow players down" so the climax was simplified.
- Source: https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary (read 2026-11-08, from search). Start players in "a visually unique room with memorable objects" as a reference point; one early puzzle "introduced too many new concepts at once" and frustrated testers; "players will rarely look up" without prompting.

### Game Maker's Toolkit and invisible tutorials
- Source: https://amara.org/v/C3BGB (read 2026-11-08, transcript listing of "Half-Life 2's Invisible Tutorial"). Half-Life 2 has no traditional tutorial; the only on-screen text is the move, jump and shoot keys; characters explain devices in dialogue.
- Source: https://amara.org/subtitles/JcxvgxWY0awb/en/1/download/Can%20We%20Make%20Better%20Tutorials%20for%20Complex%20Games.en.txt (read 2026-11-08). Brown: "you can sometimes teach players without having a tutorial at all"; Portal's early chambers are each "inferring a small piece of information about how the game works"; delaying tutorials works when designers "slowly introduce game mechanics throughout the course of the adventure".
- Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/methods-of-creating-invisible-tutorials (read 2026-11-08; Alex Pine, 2019). Five methods: affordances ("cues in design that indicate how something is used"), skill gates that "stop the player from progressing" until a skill is shown, demonstration before interaction, trial and error ("just let the player make a mistake and learn from it") with cheap retries, and context-sensitive help after seconds of inactivity.

### Zach Gage, "Building Games That Can Be Understood at a Glance" (GDC 2018)
- Source: https://pixelpoppers.com/link/361a02/building-games-that-can-be-understood-at-a-glance/ (read 2026-11-08; video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YISKcRDcDJg). ### Raph Koster, A Theory of Fun
- Source: https://www.raphkoster.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Theory-of-Fun-10-Years-Later.pdf (read 2026-11-08, from search; file too large to fetch). Koster: "Fun is just another word for learning"; "games are systems built to help us learn patterns"; fun is "a neurochemical reward to encourage us to keep trying".
- Source: https://www.shortform.com/blog/what-is-the-meaning-of-fun/ (read 2026-11-08; paraphrase, not Koster's words). ### Steam demos and Next Fest (Chris Zukowski and others)
- Source: https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=2590 (read 2026-11-08; "What is a good median play time for a demo? [Benchmark]", 2022-10-26). Survey of ~130 games: median 14 minutes, average 19.75, 30th percentile 10, 70th percentile 22; by revenue tier Bronze 7, Silver 18, Gold 38, Diamond 65 minutes. Developers designed ~30-minute demos but "the average player will not play your game for the entire time you designed it for"; the question is "whether people want to play through it to the end"; check whether the tutorial is "too long" or "too short"; one demo's median rose from 15 to 42 minutes after easing default difficulty.
- Source: https://howtomarketagame.com/2021/05/10/keep-your-free-steam-demo-up-forever/ (read 2026-11-08). "Your demo shouldn't just be the first 30 minutes of your game"; "It is more like a playable trailer"; "You want people to leave wanting more"; players who felt they had "seen enough" wishlisted less; "ALWAYS HAVE A CALL TO ACTION!"; "Always show your work and get feedback ASAP."
- Source: https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/how-long-should-a-steam-demo-be (read 2026-11-08; secondary synthesis). Time to first hook "Under 10 minutes" labelled community consensus, "inside 10 minutes, ideally inside 5"; a median under 10 minutes is "a warning light, not a length choice"; Steamworks' only guidance is "You need to balance giving the customer enough content to get them excited".
- Source: https://wnhub.io/news/other/item-1822 (read 2026-11-08, from search). Zukowski on timing: "You should not be debuting your demo during Next Fest"; release the demo well before the event and keep it up.

### Strategy and board-game criteria
- Source: https://gamedeveloper.com/design/criteria-for-strategy-game-design (read 2026-11-08; Fabian Fischer, 2014). Strategy games are "contests of decision-making"; a good decision gives enough information to guess but not to be certain; transparency: players must see how the state arose, what options exist and their immediate consequences; density of interesting decisions, with waiting, cutscenes and input-blocking animations as waste ("If players give you five minutes, that's a huge gift"); output randomness clouds whether a win came from decisions; "Should you do X?" is a decision, "Can you do X?" is execution; "easy to learn, hard to master"; theme should help players learn the rules intuitively.
- Source: https://cardboardrepublic.com/?p=3549 (read 2026-11-08). Five qualities of great board games: involvement ("there's no downtime while you wait for another player to act"), intuitive gameplay ("well-defined and logical" rules, no rulebook checks), meaningful decisions ("a game should never make players feel like their time would be better spent elsewhere"), variety, and defined purpose (the player can answer "How do I win?").
- Source: https://sites.google.com/view/daggerheartgaming/about-gaming/about-games/what-makes-a-game-good (read 2026-11-08; reproduces Wolfgang Kramer's essay). "Every player must have at least a theoretical possibility of winning until the very end"; reasonable waiting times; creative control so players are not "played by the game"; tension with several peaks, no long flat stretches; learning and mastering: quick start, clear simple rules, everyday logic; "complex sets of rules are acceptable only in conjunction with" high player influence.

## Ten-minute play-session checklist
1. 0:00 to 0:30. Can the juror state the goal and the win condition in one sentence without reading a manual? (Cardboard Republic "How do I win?"; Gage first read; Fan "eight words".)
2. 0:00 to 0:30. Does the opening screen itself draw the eye to the one thing to do first, with no wall of text and no required reading of a README? (Radiator "just put it in your game"; Portal first room.)
3. By 2:00. Has the player made a decision that visibly changed the outcome, not just clicked "next"? (Fischer decision density; Fan "perform an action once".)
4. Every action. Does the world answer it with a visible, immediate result, so cause and effect can be read without explanation? (Birdwell "if the world ignores the player"; Hodent "Signs and Feedback".)
5. By 5:00. Has the signature mechanic (the thing a juror would describe to another juror) been reached and used once? (Radiator "so we can talk about it"; Zukowski "playable trailer", not the first 30 minutes.)
6. Throughout. Is on-screen text short, in the player's language, legible at the game's default window size, and limited to what is needed now? (Fan tips 5 and 8; Ligman "explain your game simply".)
7. Throughout. Were mechanics introduced one at a time, with later systems deferred until the first ones are understood? (Fan "spread out mechanics"; Portal commentary on too many concepts at once.)
8. First failure. When the player does something wrong or loses, is the cause clear, the retry cheap, and the blame on the player's choice rather than on hidden rules? (Birdwell self-blame; Pine trial and error; Fischer transparency.)
9. Pacing. Any stretch over 30 seconds with nothing to decide (waiting, unskippable animation, loading, reading)? Count them. (Fischer waste; Kramer waiting times; Birdwell experiential density.)
10. Information. At each decision, can the player see the state, the options and their immediate consequences, with odds known where there is chance? (Fischer transparency; Cardboard Republic intuitive gameplay.)
11. Help. If the juror stalls for 10 seconds, does the game offer a context-sensitive hint rather than nothing or a modal lecture? (Fan adaptive messaging; Pine context-sensitive tutorials.)
12. Build. Did it load in a browser without an account, download or special hardware, on keyboard and mouse, in under a minute? A failure here ends most judges' sessions. (Radiator; IGF tips on web builds; IndieCade "don't submit a broken build".)
13. Hook check at 10:00. Would the juror keep playing if allowed, and can they name what would happen next? (Zukowski "leave wanting more"; Koster: is there still a pattern left to learn?)
14. Novelty. Can the juror name one thing this game does that they have not seen before, in one sentence? (IGF Grand Prize "innovation"; Nuovo "substantive risks"; A MAZE "genre pushing"; LD Innovation.)
15. Theme and mood. Does the fiction help explain the rules rather than fight them, and does the game have a recognisable mood by minute three? (Fischer theme as presentation; LD Mood; Fan "leverage what people already know".)

## Implications for Forget to Foresee
- The judge path has a hard budget. A human juror on the `games` track will give the live link roughly the IGF judge's 2 to 15 minutes, and the AI screener runs the README. The six-step README table must get a first-time player from page load to a staked prediction and a "Verified ✓" reveal inside five minutes; anything after that (memory forgetting, flying into the Shell) is a bonus the rubric should not assume a juror reaches.
- The five-beat tutorial should be judged with Fan's rules: at most about eight words per beat, each beat makes the player perform the action once (read a sign, stake silk, see the Gift land, see the proof verify, forget a memory), no beat pauses the game to lecture. Count words per beat and report it in the scorecard.
- Four-slot alien signs (Place/Action/Power/Delay) are the game's information layer; Fischer's transparency test applies directly: at the moment of staking, can the player see what each slot means, what their options are, and what a correct or wrong stake will cost? If a sign cannot be decoded without the Memories (M) panel, the first trial must say so.
- The Merkle-root "Verified ✓" is the NEAR-facing moment. Birdwell's rule says the world must answer the action: the reveal should animate the Gift landing, show the stake resolving, and only then show the proof, so that cause (the stake) and effect (the silk change) are read before the chain detail. A bare checkmark with a hash is noise by Fan's standard.
- Signature mechanic is "forget a memory to see future signs early". Checklist item 5 asks whether it is reached in a ten-minute session; with 17 trials, the first forget must be offered by trial 2 or 3, not after a long warm-up, or jurors will describe the game as a betting game rather than a foresight game.
- Keyboard hotkeys (G, T, M, V, F) are a Half-Life 2 style control layer: acceptable only if the only text on screen at first is the keys needed now. A juror who sees five letters at once has been given too much; show G first, then T, add M and V when the first sign needs them.
- Novice/Pro presentation modes map to Gage's reads: Novice is first and second read (what is happening, the high-level rules), Pro is the third read. Default must be Novice for an unknown visitor; a judge should never have to find the toggle to understand trial 1.
- Failure states: a lost stake is the game's first failure. Per Birdwell and Pine it must be cheap, explained (which slot misled the player), and immediately followed by the next sign. If a wrong stake ends the run or costs minutes, item 8 fails.
- Pacing: Kramer and Fischer penalise dead time. Measure seconds from "T end trial" to the next decision; any reveal, verification or 3D walk longer than about 30 seconds without input is a deduction, and the walk-to-goal (G) should be skippable or automatic in Novice mode.
- Discoverability lessons from LD (20 ratings minimum, browser build, Coolness) and IGF (judges skip slow or internet-only builds) translate to: the live link must load in a browser without wallet setup for the first trial, and the README must state the current state of the build and the expected play time, as the IGF tips demand.
- Novelty sentence for the jury (item 14): "stake on alien signs, verify the reveal against a Merkle root, then pay with memory to see the future" is one sentence; the demo post should lead with it, as the hackathon's own guidance asks and as Day of the Devs' "what is new and exciting" factor rewards.
